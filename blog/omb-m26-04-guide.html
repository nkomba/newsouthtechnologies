Understanding OMB M-26-04: A Practical Guide for Federal AI Procurement

The new 'Unbiased AI Principles' mandate a shift from 'move fast and break things' to 'verify before you deploy.' Here is what contractors need to demonstrate and what CIOs need to verify.

Eugene P. Nkomba Co-Managing Partner, New South Technologies

The Era of "Move Fast and Break Things" Is Over for Federal AI

In late 2025, the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) issued Memorandum M-26-04, titled Guidance on Unbiased AI Principles for Federal Agencies. While the title sounds academic, the impact is immediate and profound: it fundamentally changes how federal agencies can procure, deploy, and operate Artificial Intelligence systems.

For decades, the tech industry has operated under a mantra of rapid iteration. If a model had a bug, patch it in production. If it hallucinated, retrain it next week. In the commercial sector, this works. In the federal space, where decisions affect benefits, parole, healthcare, and national security, that approach is no longer acceptable.

M-26-04's core requirement is narrower and more specific than "all AI must be governed": the large language models a federal agency procures must be truth-seeking and free from encoded ideological bias. The wider disciplines people associate with responsible AI — explainability, bias testing, and post-deployment monitoring — come from the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and related guidance, and are best practice we apply alongside M-26-04 rather than mandates of the memo itself.

For IT leaders and contractors in the Washington D.C. metro area, this isn't just a policy update—it's a procurement requirement. Here is what you need to know to navigate the new landscape.

What is M-26-04 Really About?

The memorandum builds upon Executive Order 14110 (Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy AI). Its specific directive centers on procuring unbiased large language models — truth-seeking, ideologically neutral, and trained on documented data. In practice, agencies pair that procurement bar with the broader NIST AI RMF governance practices below:

  1. Mandate Bias Testing: Require vendors to provide empirical evidence of bias mitigation across protected classes (race, gender, age, etc.) prior to contract award.
  2. Enforce Explainability (NIST AI RMF): No "black box" models for high-stakes decisions — the system should be able to articulate *why* it reached a conclusion. This is a NIST AI RMF expectation agencies apply alongside M-26-04, not a clause of the memo itself.
  3. Document Training Data: Vendors must disclose the provenance of training data to ensure it does not contain illegal, harmful, or politically skewed inputs.
  4. Continuous Monitoring (NIST AI RMF): Post-deployment monitoring to detect "drift" — where a model's performance degrades or becomes biased over time — is a NIST AI RMF governance practice agencies apply in tandem with M-26-04.

The key takeaway: Auditability is now a feature, not an add-on.

The Contractor's Challenge: What You Must Deliver

If your company is bidding on a federal AI project, the evaluation criteria have changed. It is no longer enough to say, "Our LLM has the highest accuracy." You must now prove:

1. The "Bias Report"

You must submit a detailed report showing how your model was tested against the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF). This includes:

  • Disaggregated testing results (e.g., performance by demographic group).
  • Documentation of any detected bias and the specific steps taken to mitigate it.
  • Statistical confidence intervals proving fairness metrics meet threshold requirements.

2. The "Explainability Matrix"

For every decision the model makes, there must be a corresponding human-readable explanation. If your AI denies a benefits claim, it must cite the specific rule and data points that led to that decision. Generic responses like "The model determined..." are no longer sufficient.

3. Data Lineage Documentation

You must trace the origin of your training data. Where did it come from? Was it scraped from the open web (risky)? Was it curated by subject matter experts (preferred)? Was it synthetic? If the data contains ideological slants, you must document how you filtered them out.

4. The "Human-in-the-Loop" Protocol

M-26-04 emphasizes that AI should augment, not replace, human judgment in critical areas. Your proposal must clearly define where a human reviewer sits in the workflow to validate high-risk outputs.

The Agency Leader's Duty: Verification, Not Just Validation

For federal CIOs and Authorizing Officials (AOs), the burden is on verification. You cannot simply accept a vendor's word that their system is "unbiased." You must:

  • Require Third-Party Audits: Engage independent assessors to validate the vendor's bias reports. Do not rely on self-certification alone.
  • Inspect the Code: Ensure the explainability layer is hardcoded into the model, not just a UI overlay. The model itself must be capable of generating explanations.
  • Monitor Continuously: Implement automated scripts that run monthly bias checks on live traffic. If the model drifts, it must automatically trigger a suspension of service.

The Hidden Trap: "Ideological Bias"

One of the most contentious aspects of M-26-04 is the explicit prohibition of "encoded ideological bias." This refers to the subtle ways Large Language Models (LLMs) can reflect the political or cultural biases of their training data.

In a federal context, this is critical. An AI used for policy drafting, legal research, or public communication cannot favor one political viewpoint over another. Contractors must demonstrate:

  • Use of balanced, neutral datasets.
  • Adversarial testing to try and "jailbreak" the model into biased responses.
  • Clear guidelines on how the model handles sensitive political topics (usually by refusing to opine).

How to Comply Without Killing Innovation

The natural reaction to these strict requirements is caution. Many agencies may freeze AI procurement entirely, fearing the risk. But innovation is still possible—and necessary—if approached correctly.

The solution lies in Responsible AI Governance:

  1. Start Small: Pilot AI in low-risk environments first (e.g., internal knowledge retrieval, drafting routine emails) before deploying to high-stakes citizen services.
  2. Iterate with Governance: Use a "Gated Release" process. Every sprint includes a governance checkpoint. If bias metrics slip, the feature doesn't ship.
  3. Leverage Pre-Vetted Models: Consider using government-pre-approved foundation models rather than building custom ones from scratch. This reduces the burden of initial training data vetting.

The Bottom Line

OMB M-26-04 is not a hurdle; it is a guardrail. It ensures that the federal government adopts AI responsibly, maintaining public trust and avoiding the costly mistakes that private companies often make.

For contractors, this means the days of selling "black box" AI are over. The winners will be those who can demonstrate not just intelligence, but integrity, transparency, and accountability.
