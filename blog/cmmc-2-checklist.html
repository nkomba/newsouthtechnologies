CMMC 2.0 Readiness Checklist for DoD Contractors

The rules have changed. The timeline is tightening. If you handle Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI), your ability to bid on future contracts depends on your CMMC status today. Here is your roadmap.

Eugene P. Nkomba Co-Managing Partner & Executive Coach, New South Technologies

The Clock Is Ticking

If you are a Defense Industrial Base (DIB) contractor, you know the landscape has shifted dramatically. The Department of Defense (DoD) has moved from voluntary cybersecurity practices to mandatory, enforceable certification under Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) 2.0.

Starting January 2025, and accelerating through 2026, contract awards for programs involving Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) will explicitly require CMMC Level 2 certification. No certificate? No contract. No access to the System for Award Management (SAM) for those specific scopes.

The good news: CMMC 2.0 streamlined the framework, removing redundant controls from Version 1.0 and aligning closely with NIST SP 800-171. The bad news: the assessment rigor is higher, and the penalties for non-compliance are severe.

Whether you are aiming for Level 1 (Foundational) or Level 2 (Advanced), use this checklist to assess your posture before the clock runs out.

Step 0: Determine Your Level

Before checking boxes, you must know which box you are in.

  • Level 1 (Foundational): For contractors handling only Federal Contract Information (FCI). Requires 17 basic safeguarding controls. Self-assessment annually.
  • Level 2 (Advanced): For contractors handling CUI. Requires 110 security requirements (aligned with NIST 800-171). Requires a Third Party Assessment Organization (C3PAO) assessment every three years (or government-led for critical national security info).
  • Level 3 (Expert): For contractors supporting highest risk programs. Based on NIST 800-172. Less common but strictly enforced for top-tier defense primes.

This checklist focuses primarily on Level 2, as it is the most common "make-or-break" requirement.

Phase 1: Scope Definition (The Most Critical Step)

Most contractors fail here because they scope too broadly or too narrowly. You cannot secure what you haven't defined.

☐ 1.1 Identify the Cui Boundary: Where does CUI enter your environment? Where is it stored? Where is it processed? Where is it transmitted? Map every data flow.
☐ 1.2 Define the CAGE Code Scope: Ensure your CMMC assessment covers the specific Commercial and Government Entity (CAGE) code associated with the contracts requiring CUI.
☐ 1.3 Segment Your Network: Can you prove that your CUI environment is logically separated from your unclassified business network? (e.g., VLANs, firewalls, air gaps).

Phase 2: Policy & Documentation (NIST 800-171 Alignment)

CMMC 2.0 requires not just implementation, but documentation. "If it isn't written down, it didn't happen."

☐ 2.1 Updated System Security Plan (SSP): Do you have an SSP that clearly describes your boundary, architecture, and how you implement each of the 110 controls?
☐ 2.2 Policies & Procedures: Are your policies reviewed and approved annually? Do they cover all required families (Access Control, Incident Response, Media Protection, etc.)?
☐ 2.3 Plan of Action & Milestones (POA&M): Do you have any known weaknesses? If so, are they documented in a POA&M with a clear remediation date? (Note: A few minor POA&Ms might be acceptable for Level 2, but critical ones will fail you).
☐ 2.4 Training Records: Do you have proof that *everyone* with access to the CUI environment has completed annual cybersecurity awareness training?

Phase 3: Technical Controls (The "Hardening")

These are the controls auditors will test empirically. They look for evidence, not promises.

☐ 3.1 Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA): Is MFA enabled for *all* remote access and *all* privileged accounts? (No exceptions).
☐ 3.2 Endpoint Detection & Response (EDR): Is EDR installed on all devices accessing CUI? Is it configured to send logs to a central SIEM?
☐ 3.3 Encryption at Rest and in Transit: Is CUI encrypted on laptops, servers, and databases? Is it encrypted when sent via email or file transfer (TLS 1.2+)?
☐ 3.4 Log Management: Are you generating logs for audit events? Are they protected from tampering? Are they retained for 90 days (minimum)?
☐ 3.5 Incident Response Testing: Have you actually run an Incident Response drill in the last 12 months? Do you have a record of it?
☐ 3.6 Supply Chain Risk Management: Do you know the security posture of your subcontractors who handle CUI on your behalf? (You are responsible for them too).

Phase 4: The "Ready" State for Assessment

Once you believe you are compliant, you need to prove it.

☐ 4.1 Select a C3PAO: Engage a certified third-party assessor early. Slots fill up fast. Get them to do a "Readiness Assessment" (mock audit) first.
☐ 4.2 Update SAM.gov: Ensure your representation in SAM regarding CMMC status is accurate. (Note: You cannot claim "Certified" until you pass).
☐ 4.3 Employee Onboarding: Are all employees vetted and trained *before* they get access to the system?
☐ 4.4 Physical Security: Is physical access to the CUI environment controlled and monitored? (Badge readers, cameras, sign-in logs).

Common Pitfalls That Cause Failure

In our experience auditing contractors, these are the top reasons organizations fail their CMMC assessment:

  • "Shadow IT": Using personal USB drives, unapproved cloud storage (Dropbox, personal Google Drive), or unregistered SaaS tools to share CUI.
  • Legacy Systems: Running Windows 7 or Server 2008 R2 systems that cannot support modern encryption or patching requirements.
  • Lack of Evidence: Being able to say "We do MFA" but not being able to show the configuration screen during the audit.
  • Subcontractor Gaps: Assuming your prime contract protects you, while your subcontractor (who actually processes the CUI) has no controls.

The Path Forward: Governed Compliance

CMMC 2.0 is not a one-time project. It is a state of continuous operations. The organizations that succeed are those that embed these controls into their daily workflow, using automation to monitor compliance rather than relying on manual checklists.

If you find yourself missing items on this list, don't panic. But do act now. The cost of retrofitting security after a breach is infinitely higher than the cost of proactive governance.
