AI Governance OMB M-26-04

Establishing Responsible AI Governance for a Federal Research Institution

From experimental ML pipelines to auditable production systems in 14 months with zero compliance setbacks.

−62% Approval Cycle Time
100% Bias Documentation
14mo Engagement Duration
45 Models Onboarded

Executive Summary

A federal research institution deploying machine learning models across 12 mission-critical programs faced growing scrutiny from oversight bodies. With no standardized governance framework, model approvals took 6–9 months, compliance documentation was manual, and two high-priority deployments were paused pending risk assessment. New South Technologies designed and implemented a responsible AI governance framework aligned with OMB M-26-04, NIST AI RMF, and the institution's existing FISMA requirements. Within 14 months, model approval cycles accelerated 62%, automated compliance evidence replaced manual reporting, and all 12 programs achieved continuous ATO status for AI workloads.

The Challenge

📋

Regulatory Uncertainty

OMB Memorandum M-26-04 had introduced new requirements for federal AI systems, including bias testing documentation, human oversight protocols, and impact assessments. The institution had no formal process to interpret or implement these requirements. Five models developed in 2024–2025 were now in regulatory limbo — not compliant, but too valuable to deactivate.

⏳

Manual Compliance Burden

Each model required a 200+ page compliance dossier assembled by hand across multiple teams (security, privacy, legal, ethics reviewers). The average approval cycle took 6–9 months. Two mission-critical forecasting models missed deployment windows because their documentation packages were rejected for missing bias metrics.

🔧

Technical Debt Accumulation

Models were deployed inconsistently across environments — some in Jupyter notebooks, others in unversioned Docker containers, and several running on legacy infrastructure without audit logging. There was no central registry of models, their owners, or their compliance status. An internal audit flagged this as "high risk" due to the inability to demonstrate accountability during an inspection.

Strategic Objectives

  • ✓ OMB M-26-04 alignment — every AI system documented, assessed, and approved according to the memorandum's requirements
  • ✓ Automated compliance evidence — audit trails generated at build time, not assembled after the fact
  • ✓ Explainability by design — all production models capable of generating human-readable decision rationales
  • ✓ Centralized model registry — single source of truth for all AI assets, owners, and compliance status
  • ✓ Continuous ATO — AI workloads maintained authorization to operate without recurring 18-month re-certifications

The Approach

01

Assessment and Framework Design

Months 1–3

New South Technologies began with a comprehensive inventory. Using automated scanning tools and stakeholder interviews, the team cataloged all 45 ML models, their deployment environments, data sources, and ownership structures. This revealed gaps:

  • 12 models had no documented bias testing
  • 8 models ran on deprecated infrastructure without audit logging
  • 6 models used proprietary algorithms with no explainability mechanism
  • 4 models lacked assigned business owners
Key decision: The team adopted the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) as the structural backbone, extending it with OMB M-26-04 compliance mappings specific to the institution's FISMA baseline.
02

Platform Foundation and Registry Build

Months 4–7

The team built a centralized model registry using open-source tooling (MLflow) deployed on the institution's FedRAMP-aligned cloud platform. The registry captured model metadata, technical specifications, compliance artifacts, and operational status.

  • Instrumented CI/CD pipelines for 6 pilot models with automated compliance checks
  • Validated training data against privacy classifications
  • Calculated fairness metrics across protected attribute groups
  • Generated SHAP/LIME explanations for model decisions

Result of Phase 2: 6 models onboarded to the registry, automated compliance documentation reduced approval preparation time from 6 weeks to 3 days.

03

Bias Mitigation and Explainability Integration

Months 8–11

For High-impact models (those affecting grant allocations, eligibility, or individual outcomes), the team implemented enhanced safeguards including pre-deployment bias audits and human-in-the-loop gates.

  • Disparate impact ratios calculated across race, gender, age, and disability categories
  • Thresholds set at <0.8 or >1.25 requiring remediation
  • Continuous drift detection alerting when model performance degraded or bias indicators shifted
Critical intervention: During bias audit of a grant eligibility model, the disparate impact ratio for rural applicants was 0.72. The team worked with data scientists to reweight features and rebalance training data, bringing the ratio to 0.89 — preventing deployment of a model that would have distributed $12M in grants inequitably.
04

Enterprise Rollout and Training

Months 12–14

With the pilot validated, the team rolled out the governance framework across all remaining 39 models:

  • Trained 23 data scientists and engineers on the new compliance pipeline and registry procedures
  • Conducted workshops for 14 program managers on interpreting fairness metrics and human oversight requirements
  • Established an AI Ethics Board (monthly meetings) to review High-impact model changes and adjudicate exceptions
  • Delivered a 120-page governance playbook and role-based training curricula

What Made This Different

Compliance was automated, not appended

Rather than bolting on documentation after model development, the governance pipeline executed checks at build time. Compliance evidence was a byproduct of engineering work, not a separate burden. This eliminated the "last 10% that takes 90% of the time" problem plaguing most federal AI initiatives.

Bias mitigation happened before deployment

The pre-deployment audit gates ensured fairness thresholds were met before models entered production. One model that would have distributed $12M in grants inequitably was corrected internally, saving the institution potential liability and reputational damage.

The framework scaled beyond technical teams

By designing for program managers, ethics reviewers, and oversight bodies — not just data scientists — the governance model became organization-wide. The Ethics Board provided sustained oversight without New South Technologies remaining on retainer indefinitely.
